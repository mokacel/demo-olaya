Walk into any supermarket and look at the olive oil section. You'll see "extra virgin" on most of them, "cold-pressed" on many, "first cold extraction" on a few. You'll see Italian flags on bottles that were bottled in Italy but pressed from Spanish, Greek, or Tunisian olives. You'll see "Product of the Mediterranean" on bottles that give no further information at all.

What you almost never see is a harvest date.

## Why the harvest date matters

Olive oil is a fresh product. It degrades. Unlike wine, it does not improve with age — it oxidises, loses polyphenols, and eventually goes rancid. A bottle of olive oil is at its best in the first six months after pressing, and should generally be used within 18 to 24 months of harvest.

Most supermarket olive oil carries a "best before" date — typically 18 to 24 months from bottling. But bottling can happen months after pressing, and pressing can happen months after harvest. The best before date tells you when the importer expects you to use it by. The harvest date tells you when the clock started.

## What to look for

A bottle that shows a harvest date — month and year — is a bottle made by a producer who is confident in their product and transparent about its age. It's not a guarantee of quality, but it's a strong signal.

The current harvest is what you want. For Northern Hemisphere producers (Spain, Italy, Greece), that means olives picked between October and January. By the time the oil reaches you, that's the freshest it gets.

## A note on "extra virgin"

Extra virgin is a legal designation with specific chemical parameters — acidity below 0.8%, peroxide levels within range, and no sensory defects. The problem is that these parameters are tested at the time of production. An oil that was genuinely extra virgin when pressed may have degraded significantly by the time it reaches the shelf.

This is why harvest date, storage conditions, and bottle type (dark glass or tin, never clear plastic) all matter as much as the grade on the label.
