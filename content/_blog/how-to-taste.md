Professional olive oil tasting uses a specific glass — small, deep blue, narrow at the top — designed to concentrate the aromas while hiding the colour. Colour is irrelevant to quality and masks other judgements, so tasters work blind to it.

You don't need the glass. But you do need to slow down.

## Warm it first

Pour a small amount into a glass and cup it in your hands for a minute or two. You're warming the oil to around 28°C, which is the temperature at which the volatile aromas are most active. Swirl it gently.

## Smell before you taste

Cover the glass and swirl again, then smell. Fresh, high-quality olive oil should smell like something — green grass, freshly cut herbs, ripe fruit, almonds, tomato leaf. Flat, musty, or rancid smells are defects.

## Take a small sip

Don't swallow immediately. Hold the oil on your tongue, then draw a small breath in through your mouth with your lips slightly parted — you'll look slightly ridiculous, but this technique, called strippaggio, vaporises the oil and opens up the flavours.

You're looking for three things: fruitiness, bitterness, and pungency.

**Fruitiness** is the positive olive aroma — the green or ripe fruit character that tells you this came from a real olive, freshly pressed.

**Bitterness** hits at the back of the tongue. In fresh, high-polyphenol oil it's a good sign — those bitter compounds are antioxidants, and their presence means the oil is genuinely fresh and unrefined.

**Pungency** is the throat sensation — the catch or burn at the back of your throat when you swallow. It's caused by oleocanthal, a polyphenol with anti-inflammatory properties. A strong catch means a high-polyphenol oil. It can be intense, almost like pepper. That's not a flaw.

## What a defect tastes like

Rancid oil tastes like old walnuts or crayons. Fusty oil — from olives that were stored too long before pressing — tastes like olives from a jar, muddy and fermented. Winey oil tastes slightly of vinegar. All of these are defects under the EVOO standard, though they're more common in commercial oils than most people realise.

## The short version

Fresh olive oil tastes alive. It smells of something. It has bitterness and a peppery finish. If an oil is completely smooth, mild, and inoffensive, that's usually because it's old, blended, or both.
